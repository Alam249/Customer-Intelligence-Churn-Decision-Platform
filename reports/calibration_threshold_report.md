# Calibration and Business Threshold Report

Generated by `scripts/run_calibration_and_threshold.py`, using the Step 9 tuned XGBoost model. All numbers are measured on the held-out test split — the calibration methods themselves are fit via cross-validation on the training split only.

## 1. Is the tuned model's probability output trustworthy?

`scale_pos_weight` (used to correct class imbalance in Step 8/9) is well known to shift predicted probabilities away from true frequencies even when it improves ranking metrics like ROC-AUC. This is checked directly, not assumed.

### Brier score (lower is better; 0 = perfect, 0.25 = a constant 0.5 guess on a balanced problem)

| probabilities | brier_score |
| --- | --- |
| Raw (tuned XGBoost) | 0.1804 |
| Calibrated (sigmoid (Platt)) | 0.1755 |
| Calibrated (isotonic) | 0.1746 |

**Decision: adopt isotonic calibration.** isotonic calibration reduces the Brier score from 0.1804 to 0.1746 — a measurable improvement, so the calibrated probabilities are used for everything below and saved as the final model.

## 4. Business cost framework

**What follows is a demonstration framework, not real company data.** Online Retail II contains no record of any actual retention campaign, its cost, or its success rate — none exists to measure. `value_per_customer` below IS measured from real data (median `monetary_total` on the training split = **899.54**, used as a simple stand-in for full customer lifetime value pending Step 12's proper CLV model). `contact_cost` and `retention_success_rate` are explicitly hypothetical assumptions, clearly labelled as such in every table below.

Cost assigned to each outcome at a threshold:

- **True Positive** (flagged an actual churner): `contact_cost - retention_success_rate * value_per_customer` — usually a net BENEFIT, since sometimes saving the customer is worth more than the outreach cost.
- **False Positive** (flagged a customer who wasn't leaving): `contact_cost` — pure waste.
- **False Negative** (missed an actual churner): `retention_success_rate * value_per_customer` — the opportunity cost of a chance not taken.
- **True Negative**: 0.

**Important limitation this framework does NOT capture**: it assumes every flagged churner has the same probability of being saved by an offer. In reality some would have stayed regardless (wasted spend beyond the FP cost already charged) and some can't be saved by any offer. That heterogeneity is exactly what Step 20's uplift modelling addresses — this is a legitimate first pass, not a substitute for it.

### Three scenarios (showing the threshold depends on the assumptions, not just the model)

| scenario | contact_cost | value_per_customer | retention_success_rate | optimal_threshold | net_value_vs_doing_nothing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary (discount code + some staff time, moderate offer) | 15.0 | 899.54 | 0.25 | 0.08 | 153709.91 |
| Cheap, low-touch (automated email/SMS, low success) | 2.0 | 899.54 | 0.12 | 0.08 | 77561.15 |
| Expensive, high-touch (personal retention call, uncertain payoff) | 120.0 | 899.54 | 0.2 | 0.36 | 55619.36 |

The optimal threshold moves from 0.08 (cheap/low-touch) to 0.36 (expensive/high-touch) purely as a function of the assumed cost and success rate — the model's probabilities don't change at all between these scenarios, only the business decision about where to act on them.

## 5. Recommended threshold for this demonstration scenario

Using the **primary scenario** (`contact_cost=€15`, `retention_success_rate=25%`, both hypothetical; `value_per_customer=€899.54`, measured): the cost-minimising threshold is **0.08**, with an estimated net value of **€153,709.91** across the 865-customer test set versus contacting no one.

At the default threshold of 0.50 instead: net value would be €103,938.50 — €49,771.41 less than the recommended threshold, a concrete demonstration of why 0.50 should not be used by default for a business decision.

**Practical caveat this number-only optimum hides**: a threshold of 0.08 flags **84.0% of all test customers** as "contact." That is mathematically optimal under the stated cost assumptions — because a low-cost offer against a much larger potential loss says "when in doubt, reach out" — but it is not a *targeted* retention list; it is closer to a mass campaign. A real retention team almost always has a capacity constraint (agent hours, a fixed offer budget) this simple framework doesn't model. In practice the threshold-performance table in section 3 or a fixed contact-list size ("top 500 customers by risk") is often the more usable operational answer; the cost-optimal threshold here is the right number for the stated assumptions, not necessarily the right number to hand a call-centre manager unmodified.

This recommendation is only as good as the three assumptions behind it — the sensitivity table above exists precisely so a reader can substitute their own numbers and see the threshold move accordingly, rather than trusting a single hard-coded answer.
